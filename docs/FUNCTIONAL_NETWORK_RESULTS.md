# Co-abundance modules recover shared cellular machinery

Co-abundance analysis grouped widely distributed KOs into recognizable cellular functions (Fig. 5). Across 191 metagenomes, the network contained 1,062 associations among 327 connected KOs. Six major modules comprised 236 KOs, of which 194 (82.2%) were near-global (Fig. 5a; Supplementary Table S14). Of the 57 KOs in M01, 17 were involved in charging tRNAs with amino acids for protein synthesis. This set was represented 3.36-fold more than expected under the conditional enrichment model (BY-adjusted P = 2.44 × 10⁻⁷; maxT P = 0.0095), with support retained when the comparison background included all connected KOs (Fig. 5b).

Other modules contained complementary parts of molecular systems. M03 included seven NADH–quinone oxidoreductase components involved in respiratory electron transfer. M06 contained 11 transporter KOs, including all five components of the reference branched-chain amino-acid uptake system (Fig. 5c,d). Across the complete matrix, the required respiratory components, allowing alternative subunit architectures, were co-detected in 176 of 191 profiles; the five uptake components co-occurred in 175. These are community-level detections, without demonstrating that all components occur in the same organism.

Enrichment supported a narrower interpretation than the component annotations alone. Neither the respiratory set nor the broad transporter category met both statistical criteria. M06 enrichment for membership of the KEGG quorum-sensing map was driven entirely by transporter genes and does not establish cell-to-cell communication. Module organization also remained sensitive to sample inclusion: omitting one six-profile BioProject reduced the major modules from six to four (Supplementary Tables S26 and S27). The network therefore identifies covarying parts of shared cellular machinery, while the atlas separately describes the geographic distribution of selected biogeochemical processes.

## Methods: Functional co-abundance networks

Network inference used all 191 functional profiles. KOs detected in at least ten profiles and contributing at least ten total annotation records defined the CLR reference of 13,308 KOs. After transformation with a pseudocount of one, network eligibility required detection in at least 20 profiles and at least 50 total records. Eligible KOs were ranked by CLR variance; the 5,000 highest-variance KOs were retained, and the top 1,500 supplied the candidate network, with ties resolved by KO identifier. Persistence classes were added after inference and did not determine candidate selection.

To limit the influence of heavily sampled localities, we generated 500 subsets of 81 metagenomes, selecting up to four profiles without replacement from each of 26 realm–country–locality groups. Associations were measured as CLR proportionality, 2cov(x,y)/(var(x) + var(y)), where x and y are the two KO profiles [55]. The 2,000 strongest absolute associations were retained per subset. Consensus edges required selection in at least 80% of subsets, complete sign consistency and mean absolute proportionality ≥0.95. Weighted Leiden clustering in igraph used resolution 1 and 50 starts (seeds 2001–2050), retaining the maximum-modularity partition of the full candidate graph [56,57]. Major modules contained at least ten KOs and were numbered by size.

Biological interpretation used a frozen KEGG reference mapping all 17,508 input KO rows to names, pathways, reactions, BRITE families and MODULE definitions [53]. This extended identifier annotation without changing sequences, counts or network membership. Multiple functional assignments were retained. Component rules preserved required subunits, alternatives and optional steps, and were evaluated within each community at thresholds of one and two annotation records. Unsupported definitions and unavailable matrix rows remained unresolved. Components within a network module were distinguished from those detected elsewhere in the matrix.

Enrichment compared each major module with their combined 236 KOs; a sensitivity analysis used all 327 connected KOs. Eligible functional sets required at least three members and three nonmembers in the primary background, giving 150 sets and 900 module–set comparisons, including zero counts. KOs were grouped into coarse matching blocks using prevalence, log-transformed total records, CLR variance, degree and absolute weighted strength. Under exchangeable module labels within these blocks, one-sided count probabilities were calculated by convolving block-specific hypergeometric distributions. Benjamini–Yekutieli adjustment covered all 900 tests [58]. An additional maxT check used 99,999 common within-block label permutations and the maximum standardized enrichment across tests. Support required both adjusted P values ≤0.05. This maxT check addresses the stated joint null; these exploratory tests are conditional on the selected graph and do not independently validate network discovery.

Module sensitivity was assessed through 500 edge perturbations and by rebuilding the network after omitting each of 43 BioProjects; profiles with unresolved project assignments were assessed separately. The CLR reference and candidate set remained fixed, with 500 locality-capped subsets and 50-start Leiden clustering per omission. Comparisons evaluated node and edge retention, module membership and mergers. Additional checks varied association thresholds and compared Leiden with Louvain [59], Infomap and Walktrap. Complete matching settings, annotation rules and numerical diagnostics are provided in the accompanying code and network supplementary information.

## Discussion

The network gives biological structure to the shared functional repertoire by grouping genes involved in protein synthesis, respiratory electron transfer and nutrient uptake (Fig. 5). The clearest enrichment, tRNA aminoacylation in M01, concerns a basic requirement for producing proteins. The Nuo components in M03 and the Liv uptake system in M06 provide complementary examples of energy conservation and resource acquisition, although their grouping remained descriptive. Their covariation could arise as organisms carrying these systems change in relative representation. This connects the shared repertoire to cellular requirements that can recur across taxonomically different communities; it does not imply faster growth or coordinated gene expression.

Amino-acid uptake offers a plausible ecological connection to sediment organic matter. In deep Costa Rica sediments, Farag et al. identified a Lokiarchaeota genome encoding amino-acid transport, including branched-chain substrates, together with pathways for amino-acid utilization [27]. In mangrove sediments, Du et al. combined genomes, transcriptomes and metabolic models to predict amino-acid exchange among community members [28]. These studies place transport within a broader context of resource use and potential exchange. Our Liv components identify uptake capacity, but cannot distinguish amino acids obtained from detritus from those released by other organisms, or determine whether imported substrates support biomass synthesis or energy metabolism. Likewise, Nuo components identify part of an electron-transfer system without specifying the terminal electron acceptor.

This distinction matters for interpreting ecosystem processes. Stable-isotope experiments in marine sediments found protein-utilization genes in both archaeal populations that incorporated labelled protein and populations that did not [29]. Encoded capacity therefore needs activity measurements before it can be linked to carbon or nitrogen turnover. In MAGENTA, the network contributes evidence that shared potential is organized into recognizable cellular systems, complementing the atlas's comparison of widely recovered and more localized biogeochemical components. Sensitivity to BioProject omission limits generalization of the exact module boundaries. Genome-resolved comparisons could test whether these associations reflect shared carriers, while transcript and substrate measurements could establish when the corresponding systems contribute to sediment functioning.
